# ADR 0107 — Paying for an extension: the shop, the issuing, the document

**Status:** Accepted — 2026-09 — *decided now, built before the first sale*

## Context

[ADR 0102](0102-paid-extensions-and-the-pixel-boundary.md) §5 fixed the
*shape* of a paid extension's licence in five lines: offline, a signed
document, a public key compiled into the extension, no network ever, and no
pretence of being unforgeable by the person running the binary.

[ADR 0073](0073-external-mask-detectors.md)'s socket now has its first
occupant — `leyline-assist`, sky and subject detection, delivered
2026-09-01 in its own private repository. So the question stops being
hypothetical: between "someone wants to pay" and "the executable agrees to
run" there are three things nobody has written down.

* **Being paid.** A digital good sold to a consumer in the European Union
  owes VAT **in the buyer's country**, from the first euro, with no
  threshold. That is a registration, a quarterly filing and twenty-seven
  rates — for a project whose author is one person.
* **Issuing.** Something must turn a completed order into a signed
  document, and that something holds the private key.
* **Verifying.** The format, and what precisely is signed.

Only the third is a technical question, and it is the easiest of the three.

## Decision

### 1. A merchant of record sells, not the author

The shop is **Paddle**: it is the *seller of record*, which means it — not
the project's author — is the party that collects VAT, files it, issues the
invoice, and absorbs refunds and chargebacks.

The commission (~5 % + fixed fee) buys the removal of a **recurring
obligation**, not a convenience. Stripe in direct charges a third of that
and leaves the whole tax apparatus on the author, every quarter, for as
long as a single licence is sold. A one-person project does not take on a
permanent quarterly obligation to save three percent of an unknown revenue.

What is decided here is the **model**, not the price: a **one-off
purchase**, never a subscription. `specification.md` §4's sentence — the
application "is not sold by subscription, does not expire and holds no
licence check" — stays true of the application, and the extension does not
quietly introduce the thing the row refuses either.

### 2. The private key never leaves the author's machine

Issuing is a local tool, `leyline-licence`, living in the extension's
private repository and **not shipped in the extension's binary**. It reads
the shop's completed orders, signs one document per order, and mails it.
It is run by hand, or by a timer on the same machine.

The cost is a delivery measured in minutes rather than seconds. What that
buys is the property that matters here: **there is no machine on the
internet that can mint a licence.** The alternative — a webhook service
signing on receipt — puts the private key on a host that is reachable,
patched by nobody in particular, and whose compromise produces valid
licences *forever*, since §5 gives us no revocation.

The key pair is generated by `leyline-licence keygen`, lives at
`~/.config/leyline/leyline-licence.key` in mode 600, and is **distinct from
the updater key** ([ADR 0077](0077-application-updates.md) §1): one key,
one job — an updater key that also signed licences would make the release
pipeline a licence-forging tool.

That key is now a thing to **back up**. Losing it does not invalidate a
single licence already issued; it means no new one can be issued, and a
replacement key means a new build of the extension.

### 3. The licence is one document, pasteable, and it says what it is

```text
----- BEGIN LEYLINE LICENCE -----
product: leyline-assist
line: 0.1
holder: Jeanne Martin <jeanne@example.org>
order: pdl_01J8Z0
issued: 2026-09-01
serial: 3f9c1a4e2b70d581
sig: 6b1f…(128 hex characters)
----- END LEYLINE LICENCE -----
```

Three properties, each of them deliberate.

* **It is readable.** A customer, and the author reading a support mail,
  can see what was bought and when. An opaque blob would hide exactly the
  information a licence dispute is about.
* **It is one block.** It survives a mail, a copy and a paste. A minisign
  pair — a payload file *plus* a `.minisig` — is the format the repository
  already uses for packages, and it is the wrong shape for something a
  human carries: two files, one of which is meaningless alone. The
  *primitive* is nonetheless the same: **Ed25519**, exactly what minisign
  signs with.
* **What is signed is not what was received.** The verifier parses the
  fields, **re-serializes them canonically**, and checks the signature
  against those bytes. A document therefore has exactly one signable form:
  spacing, field order and trailing whitespace cannot become a second
  reading of the same licence.

`activate` writes the document into the extension's configuration
directory; `LEYLINE_ASSIST_LICENCE` overrides the path, which is what makes
it testable and what a packaged deployment uses.

### 4. It is verified in the extension, and nowhere else

The check lives in `leyline-assist`. Leyline contains no licence code,
calls no verification, and does not know whether a detector was paid for —
[ADR 0102](0102-paid-extensions-and-the-pixel-boundary.md) §4, unchanged.

The socket's behaviour without a licence is chosen so that the user learns
something:

* the **manifest is still emitted**, so Studio lists the detector rather
  than making it vanish;
* a **detect call refuses**, with one `stderr` line naming both the reason
  and the remedy.

That line reaches the user verbatim: `leyline-detect` passes a failing
detector's `stderr` through unaltered (`DetectError::Failed`,
`crates/leyline-detect/src/lib.rs`). The socket needs no change — a licence
refusal is an ordinary detector failure, which is the whole point of having
built the socket that way.

### 5. Perpetual, per line, with no clock and no revocation

A licence names a **line** (`0.1`, later `1`, `2`) and never expires. The
binary compares the line against its own version and that is the entire
check: **no date is consulted**, because an offline verifier cannot
distinguish a wrong clock from a dishonest one, and a licence that stops
working on a machine whose battery died would be exactly the "right to run
what you have" the project refuses to sell.

A new line is a new purchase, at an upgrade price. Everything already
bought keeps working, on that line, forever.

There is **no revocation list**: it would require a network call. A
refunded licence therefore stays valid. That is an accepted cost — the
merchant of record carries the fraud and chargeback side, which is
precisely one of the things §1 pays for.

### 6. The trial is a courtesy, and the whole system is honest about itself

Without a licence the extension performs **twenty detections**, counted in
a local file, then refuses. The counter is erasable by anyone who finds it,
and it is not hidden.

This is [ADR 0102](0102-paid-extensions-and-the-pixel-boundary.md) §5's
statement applied, and it deserves to be restated in the terms of what was
actually built:

* **Forging a licence is out of reach.** It requires the private key;
  Ed25519 gives that property and nothing about the check being local
  weakens it.
* **Skipping the check is not.** Anyone can patch the binary they were
  given, and every countermeasure — obfuscation, integrity self-checks,
  hardware binding, phoning home — is refused, both because they do not
  work against a determined person and because they would make the paid
  edition behave in ways the free one exists to reject.

The licence marks the honest customer. That is what it is for.

### 7. What ships beside the binary

Two files, both in the extension's repository, both required before a first
sale:

* an **EULA** — a proprietary licence must state its terms; the GPLv3 §7
  additional permission of [ADR 0069](0069-closed-extension-boundary.md) §4
  authorizes the combination, it does not license the extension;
* a **NOTICE** carrying the third-party attributions the shipped weights
  require (U²-Net, Apache-2.0; skyseg, MIT). Selling a bundle does not
  remove an attribution clause, and the licences of both models were
  already the eliminating criterion of ADR 0073 §5.

### 8. Decided now, built before the first sale

`leyline-assist` ships **with no check at all** for the time being (decided
2026-09-01): no licence code, no trial counter, no embedded key. The
detectors work for whoever installs them.

That is deliberate and it costs nothing, because everything above is
**additive and entirely inside the extension**. Nothing in this repository
is waiting on it (§Consequences), the socket needs no change (§4), and the
document format is fixed here rather than improvised the week of a launch —
which is the whole reason to decide it while nobody is in a hurry.

What must be true the day it is built, and this is the checklist:

* the key pair is generated **fresh** (§2), backed up, and distinct from the
  updater key;
* the public key is compiled into the extension, so adding the check is a
  **new release** of the extension and never a configuration change;
* the free application is not touched — if a change to this repository ever
  looks necessary to make the licence work, the design has drifted from
  ADR 0102 §4 and it is the design that is wrong;
* the EULA and NOTICE of §7 exist **before** the first sale, not after.

## Consequences

* **Nothing changes in this repository.** No crate, no engine surface, no
  new dependency; `specification.md` §4 already says what stays true.
* The extension gains a startup cost of a few hundred microseconds — one
  file read and one signature verification — against detections measured in
  seconds.
* The author gains **one secret to keep and back up**, and an issuing step
  that is a command rather than a service.
* Should the volume ever make the manual step painful, §2's tool is what
  gets scheduled more often; the design does not change, only its cadence.

## Alternatives rejected

* **Stripe in direct** — cheapest, and it makes the author the seller of
  record: EU VAT registration, quarterly filings, per-country rates,
  indefinitely. Rejected in §1: a permanent obligation is not worth three
  percent.
* **A webhook service that signs on receipt** — seconds instead of minutes,
  at the price of the private key living on an internet-facing host, with
  no revocation to fall back on (§5). The gain is convenience; the loss is
  the one property the whole design rests on.
* **A pool of pre-signed anonymous licences** delivered by the shop —
  instant, key offline, and it removes the holder's name, which is the only
  deterrent against passing a licence around. Rejected: it buys latency
  with the sole thing §6 relies on.
* **Online activation**, already rejected by ADR 0102 §5 and `vision.md`,
  and re-rejected here for a second reason: it would make the extension
  stop working the day the author stops operating the service.
* **Hardware binding / node locking** — punishes the honest customer who
  changes machine, and is defeated by the same patch that defeats the
  signature check. All cost, no property.
* **An expiring licence** — needs a clock the verifier cannot trust, and
  §5's principle refuses it independently.
* **A licence check in Leyline itself**, even one that merely reads a flag —
  ADR 0102 §4's red line: the free application must contain no licence code
  at all, so that no future version can be accused of having grown one.
